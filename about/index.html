About

When to step outside and look up, for anywhere on Earth.

What it works out

The space stations are only visible when they are still in sunlight high up while your sky is dark, usually in the two hours after sunset or before sunrise. SkyPassAlert follows the International Space Station and China’s Tiangong station from their published orbits, finds those moments for your place, and says in plain words which way to face, how high and how bright. It also lists the Moon, the bright planets and meteor showers for the night.

All of the math runs on your phone. Nothing is sent to a server.

How accurate it is

Every change is checked automatically against Skyfield, a standard astronomy library. Largest differences measured:

Station position 14.9 m
Rise and set times 0.27 s
Sunset 3 s
Moon position 5.9′

Fifteen real passes over Fremont, California were also compared with Heavens-Above: the times matched to within 2 seconds. The real limit is the orbit data itself. Stations change their orbits, so passes more than about 3 days away can shift by minutes. The page always shows how old its orbit data is. Full report.

Meteor rates are rougher: they are estimates from each shower’s typical strength, the height of its radiant and the Moon, and real showers vary from year to year.

Who makes it

A high-school student who wanted to know when to take the family outside to look up, with help from AI coding tools. Corrections and ideas are welcome.

The fine print

These are predictions for sky-watching, not guarantees. Clouds, trees, buildings and streetlights all decide what you can actually see. Not for navigation or any safety-critical use.
